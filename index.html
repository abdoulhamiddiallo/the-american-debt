Six signals to remember

The observatory's summary

A debt as large as the economyDebt held by the public reached about 99% of GDP at the end of fiscal year 2026 (CBO: about 100%), its highest since 1946. Only the end of World War II was higher (106.3%). CBO expects a new record (108%) around 2030.
No surplus since 2001Twenty-five deficits in a row. The FY2026 deficit, $1.99 trillion or 6.2% of GDP, is the largest of any advanced economy, in a year of full employment.
Interest has crossed $1 trillionAbout $1.09 trillion in fiscal year 2026, triple the 2021 level. Interest now exceeds national defense and Medicare, and makes up more than half of the deficit.
Markets demand moreThe 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.28% on October 7, its highest since 2002. The 30-year bond trades at 5.67%. Every refinanced dollar now costs more.
A third of the debt rolls over within a year$10.6 trillion of marketable securities mature in fiscal year 2027, including $7.1 trillion of Treasury bills, repriced at today's rates.
The dollar is the safety netThe United States borrows in its own currency, the world's reserve currency, in the deepest bond market on earth. That is why it can carry this debt, and why the risk is a slow squeeze rather than a sudden crisis.

Sustainable for now, on an unsustainable path

The United States is not on the edge of default: it borrows in its own currency, which the world uses for trade and savings, and the demand for Treasuries remains enormous. But every arrow points the wrong way: a deficit of 6% in good times, an interest bill above $1 trillion, market yields above the average rate paid, and a political process that turns the debt limit into a recurring crisis.

The danger is not a number. It is the moment interest grows faster than the economy's ability to pay it.

The equation everyone watches

Δdebt = primary deficit
+ (rate − growth) × debt

In fiscal year 2026, inflation helped: nominal growth (about 5.8%) was well above the average rate paid on the debt (3.5%), which erased more than five points of GDP. That is why the ratio rose by only about one point despite a $2 trillion deficit. But new borrowing now costs 4% to 5.7%, and CBO expects growth near 4%: the help is fading.

The snowball effect: once the average rate climbs above growth, the debt grows on its own, even with a balanced primary budget.

The exorbitant privilege

The world's reserve currency. About 58% of central bank reserves are held in dollars. Foreign governments, banks and companies need Treasuries, which keeps U.S. borrowing costs lower than its fiscal numbers alone would justify.
Its own central bank. The debt is in dollars, which the Federal Reserve issues. A technical default on payments is a political choice, not a market accident, which is why debt-limit standoffs worry the agencies so much.
A privilege, not a guarantee. If investors start to doubt fiscal discipline, they ask for a higher term premium on long bonds. With 10-year yields above 5% and inflation back above target, that question is now being asked.

Estimate: Treasury debt on October 7, 2026, advanced at the FY2026 average pace (about $6.9 billion a day). The debt subject to limit is slightly below the total. Once the limit is reached, Treasury uses "extraordinary measures" that buy several months before the X-date, when it could no longer pay every bill on time.

How a standoff unfolds

1The limit is reached. Treasury can no longer issue new net debt. Auctions continue only to roll over maturing securities.
2Extraordinary measures. Treasury suspends investments of some federal retirement funds and draws down its cash account to keep paying bills.
3The X-date. When measures and cash run out, Treasury cannot pay all obligations: Social Security checks, salaries, bondholders. It has never happened.
4The deal. Congress raises or suspends the limit, often at the last minute, and Treasury rebuilds its cash with a burst of bill issuance.

Why it matters for the cost of debt

Ratings. Two of the three downgrades (2011 and 2023) followed debt-limit standoffs. Agencies see them as a sign of weak governance.
Market turbulence. Bills maturing near an X-date trade at higher yields, and the scramble to rebuild Treasury's cash after a deal drains liquidity from markets.
A limit on borrowing, not on spending. The ceiling caps the financing of spending and tax laws that Congress has already passed. Raising it does not authorize new spending.

What it means

China's debt rises faster, from a lower level, and costs little: its state banks lend to the state at low rates in a closed system, and interest takes about 1% of GDP. Part of the borrowing done by local governments sits outside the official figures, so its true debt is higher still.

The United States pays 3.6% of GDP in interest, three and a half times more, because it borrows at market rates from investors around the world. In exchange, it enjoys a privilege China does not have: the whole world wants its bonds.

In one sentence: China's debt is a domestic problem it controls, at the cost of growth; America's debt is a global asset whose price markets reset every day.

Analysis note · October 2026

The U.S. national debt: position, dynamics and risks

Data as of October 9, 2026 · Sources: U.S. Treasury, Congressional Budget Office, OMB, Federal Reserve, IMF, Census Bureau

1.Summary

The gross federal debt of the United States reached $40,171.8 billion at the end of fiscal year 2026 (September 30), up $2,534.2 billion in twelve months. Debt held by the public, the measure economists use, stood at $32,425.0 billion, about 99% of gross domestic product by our estimate (CBO: about 100%), its highest since 1946; only the end of World War II was higher (106.3%). The United States remains solvent and finances itself without difficulty, but its trajectory is the most worrying among large advanced economies: a deficit of 6.2% of GDP in a year of full employment, an interest bill that has crossed $1 trillion and market yields at their highest since 2002.

2.Position at the end of fiscal year 2026

Of the gross debt, $7,746.8 billion is held inside the government, mostly by the Social Security and federal retirement trust funds, and $32,425.0 billion by the public. Marketable securities total $31,834.9 billion: notes ($16,281.5 billion), bills ($7,118.1 billion), bonds ($5,551.0 billion), inflation-protected securities ($2,172.6 billion) and floating rate notes ($708.0 billion). Among holders, foreign investors account for about $9.25 trillion (July 2026), the Federal Reserve for $4.56 trillion and U.S. investors for the rest.

In fiscal year 2026, according to CBO, the federal government spent $7.40 trillion (23.1% of GDP) and collected $5.40 trillion (16.8%). Out of $1,000 of program spending in fiscal year 2025, $220 went to Social Security, $139 to Medicare, $136 to Medicaid and other health programs, $135 to net interest and $128 to national defense. Including states and localities, U.S. government spending (37.7% of GDP, IMF) is lower than in most European countries, but revenue (30.9%) ranks 24th of 33 major economies.

3.A structurally rising debt

The federal budget has not been in surplus since fiscal year 2001. The debt rises in steps during crises (2001, 2008-2009, 2020) and never comes back down. Since 2022 it has kept climbing without any crisis, with deficits above 5% of GDP at full employment. The pace has accelerated: the debt went from $30 trillion (January 2022) to $40 trillion (August 18, 2026) in four and a half years, and the latest trillion took 154 days.

In fiscal year 2026, the ratio of debt held by the public rose only from 98.1% to 99.3% of GDP despite a $2 trillion deficit: the primary deficit added 2.8 points and interest 3.4 points, but nominal growth of about 5.8%, boosted by inflation, removed about 5.4 points. This favorable effect depends on growth staying well above the average rate paid on the debt, which is no longer the case for new borrowing.

4.The cost of debt

Net interest rose from $352 billion in fiscal year 2021 to $970 billion in fiscal year 2025 and about $1,085 billion in fiscal year 2026 (3.4% of GDP, above the previous record of 3.2% in 1991), more than national defense or Medicare, and second only to Social Security. It now absorbs about one dollar in five of federal revenue and accounts for more than half of the deficit. The average rate on interest-bearing debt climbed from 1.61% (September 2021) to 3.53% (September 2026). By our estimate, a lasting one-point rise in rates would add about $100 billion of interest in the first year and more than $300 billion a year after five years. CBO projects net interest at $2.1 trillion (4.6% of GDP) in 2036.

5.Markets and ratings

The 10-year Treasury yield reached 5.28% on October 7, 2026 (5.31% on October 5), its highest level since 2002, and the 30-year bond 5.67%, as inflation returned above 3% (3.4% in August), an oil shock lifted prices and the Federal Reserve raised its policy rate in September. The yield curve now slopes upward, a sign that investors demand a higher premium to lend for longer. Demand at auctions remains strong, with bid-to-cover ratios of 2.4 to 2.6. All three agencies rate the United States one notch below the top: S&P (AA+, since 2011), Fitch (AA+, since 2023) and Moody's (Aa1, since May 2025), each with a stable outlook.

6.Financing and creditors

Treasury borrowed a net $2.15 trillion from the public in fiscal year 2026 and auctioned about $28.6 trillion of securities in gross terms, mostly bills rolled over every few weeks. The maturity wall is high: $10.6 trillion of marketable debt matures in fiscal year 2027, including $7.1 trillion of bills, and $21.7 trillion within five years. Foreign holdings reached a record $9.49 trillion in February 2026 before easing to $9.25 trillion in July. Japan is the largest foreign holder ($1.10 trillion), ahead of the United Kingdom ($998 billion) and China ($618 billion, less than half its 2013 peak). The Federal Reserve has reduced its Treasury holdings by about $1.2 trillion since 2022.

7.International comparisons

According to the IMF, U.S. general government debt (123.9% of GDP in 2025) ranks fourth among 33 major economies, behind Japan (206.5%), Greece (145.7%) and Italy (137.1%). Its deficit (-6.8%) is the largest of any advanced economy, and only Brazil, China, India and Poland run larger ones among the 33. The IMF projects U.S. debt at 142.1% of GDP in 2031, the steepest increase in the G7, while Greece, Portugal, Spain and Japan reduce their ratios. China's debt rises even faster (from 99.2% to 126.8%), but in a closed domestic system with low interest costs.

Under current law, CBO projects debt held by the public to break the 1946 record around 2030 (108% of GDP) and reach 120% in 2036, 144% in 2046 and 175% in 2056. Two trust-fund deadlines frame the debate: the Highway Trust Fund in fiscal year 2028 and the Social Security retirement trust fund in fiscal year 2032, which would trigger an automatic benefit cut of about 28% without new legislation. The debt limit, raised to $41.1 trillion in July 2025, is expected to bind again in 2027.

9.Main risks

Rates: new borrowing now costs more than the average paid on the debt, so the interest bill will keep rising even without new deficits. Growth and inflation: the inflation that shrank the ratio in 2022-2026 also pushes rates up; a recession would widen the deficit and make the ratio jump. Rollover: a third of marketable debt matures within a year. Politics: debt-limit standoffs and the absence of a bipartisan fiscal plan weigh on ratings and on the term premium. Our Monte Carlo simulation, without a change in policy, puts median debt held by the public around 126% of GDP in 2036, above CBO's 120%, because it includes the risk of recessions.

10.Conclusion

The American debt is not on the brink of a crisis: the United States borrows in its own currency, the world's reserve currency, in the deepest bond market on earth. But every indicator of dynamics is deteriorating at once, in a period of full employment that should have been used to rebuild margins. The tipping point is not a given debt level: it is the moment interest grows faster than the economy's ability to pay it. The United States is moving toward it, and only Congress and the President can change the primary balance, the one variable they control directly.

Gross debt and debt held by the public

Gross federal debt counts everything the Treasury owes, including securities held by federal trust funds such as Social Security. Debt held by the public excludes those internal holdings: it is what the government owes to investors, the Federal Reserve and foreign holders, and the measure most economists use.

Deficit

The yearly gap between outlays and revenue. A deficit of 6% of GDP means borrowing 6% of everything the economy produces to close the year.

Primary deficit

The deficit before interest: the part of the budget that Congress and the President control directly.

Debt limit and X-date

The legal cap on federal borrowing ($41.1 trillion since July 2025). The X-date is the day Treasury would run out of cash and extraordinary measures and could no longer pay every bill on time.

Snowball effect

When the average interest rate exceeds nominal growth, the debt ratio rises on its own, even with a balanced primary budget.

Bills, notes, bonds, TIPS

Bills mature in one year or less; notes in 2 to 10 years; bonds in 20 or 30 years. TIPS are notes and bonds whose principal is indexed to consumer prices.

Basis point and term premium

A basis point is 0.01 percentage point. The term premium is the extra yield investors demand to lend for a long time rather than roll over short loans.

Bid-to-cover ratio

The total amount bid at an auction divided by the amount sold. A ratio of 2.5 means investors offered to buy two and a half times what Treasury sold.

Fiscal year

The federal fiscal year runs from October 1 to September 30: fiscal year 2026 ended on September 30, 2026.

Monte Carlo simulation

A method that draws thousands of random scenarios to estimate a probability rather than a single number. The IMF uses it in its debt sustainability analyses.
